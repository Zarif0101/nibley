# Haloalkanes

## Simple idea
A haloalkane is an alkane where one hydrogen is swapped for a halogen such as chlorine or bromine. The halogen pulls electrons harder than carbon, so the carbon turns slightly positive. A particle with a spare pair of electrons (a nucleophile) is attracted to it, kicks the halogen out and takes its place.

## Key formulas
- CH₃CH₂Br + OH⁻ → CH₃CH₂OH + Br⁻. Substitution with aqueous hydroxide
- Nucleophile: a particle that donates a lone pair to a δ+ carbon
- Bond strength: C–F > C–Cl > C–Br > C–I, so iodoalkanes react fastest
- Ag⁺(aq) + Br⁻ → AgBr (cream solid). Silver nitrate test for the halide

## Common mistakes
- Thinking the most polar bond reacts fastest: C–Cl is most polar but C–I is weakest, so C–I reacts fastest.
- Mixing up conditions: aqueous hydroxide gives substitution, ethanolic hydroxide gives an alkene.
- Calling the attacker an electrophile: it donates electrons, so it is a nucleophile.

## Check questions
1. What forms when 1-bromopropane reacts with aqueous NaOH?
   Answer: Propan-1-ol (and sodium bromide).
2. Which reacts faster, chloroalkane or iodoalkane?
   Answer: The iodoalkane.
3. Why is the carbon in a C–Br bond attacked by nucleophiles?
   Answer: Bromine is more electronegative, so the carbon is slightly positive.

## Flashcards
Q: What is a nucleophile?
A: A lone-pair donor that attacks a slightly positive carbon.
Q: What is the colour of silver bromide?
A: Cream.
Q: Which carbon-halogen bond is the weakest?
A: C–I.
Q: What does ethanolic KOH do to a haloalkane?
A: Removes H and halogen to make an alkene.
Q: What is the type of reaction when OH⁻ replaces Br?
A: Nucleophilic substitution.

## Root check
- hydrocarbons: What is an alkene? Answer: A hydrocarbon with a C=C double bond.
