# Sound waves

## Simple idea
When a speaker cone moves, it squashes the air in front and stretches it behind. Those squashed and stretched patches travel outward, and your ear feels them as sound. The air itself only wobbles back and forth. Faster wobbling gives a higher pitch, and a bigger push gives a louder sound.

## Key formulas
- v = f·λ. v: speed of sound (about 340 m/s in air), f: frequency (hertz, Hz), λ: wavelength (m)
- Humans hear roughly 20 Hz to 20 000 Hz

## Picture it
Draw `wave-longitudinal`. Label `wave-longitudinal.compression` "compression: squashed air" and `wave-longitudinal.rarefaction` "rarefaction: stretched air".
Arrow along `wave-longitudinal.right` labelled "sound travels".

## Common mistakes
- Thinking sound travels in space: it needs matter, so not in a vacuum.
- Mixing pitch and loudness: pitch is frequency, loudness is amplitude.
- Drawing sound as up-and-down: air wobbles along the travel direction.

## Check questions
1. A sound has f = 680 Hz in air. What is its wavelength? (v = 340 m/s)
   Answer: 340 / 680 = 0.5 m.
2. Which has a higher frequency: a whistle or a drum?
   Answer: The whistle, since it has a higher pitch.
3. Why can you not hear sound in space?
   Answer: There is no air to carry the squashing and stretching.

## Flashcards
Q: What are the two parts of a sound wave?
A: Compressions and rarefactions.
Q: What sets the pitch?
A: The frequency.
Q: What sets the loudness?
A: The amplitude.
Q: Speed of sound in air?
A: About 340 m/s.
Q: Can sound travel through a vacuum?
A: No.
