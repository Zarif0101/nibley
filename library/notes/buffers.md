# Buffer solutions

## Simple idea
Think of a sponge that soaks up spills. A buffer holds a weak acid and its partner base side by side. Add a little acid and the base mops it up. Add a little base and the acid cancels it. So the pH hardly moves, which is how your blood stays safe.

## Key formulas
- pH = pKa + log([A⁻] / [HA]). pKa: −log Ka (no unit), [A⁻]: salt (base) concentration (mol/dm³), [HA]: weak acid concentration (mol/dm³)
- Add acid: A⁻ + H⁺ → HA
- Add alkali: HA + OH⁻ → A⁻ + H₂O

## Common mistakes
- Using a strong acid with its salt: a buffer needs a weak acid and its salt.
- Thinking a buffer stops pH change completely: it only resists small additions.
- Flipping the ratio in the formula: it is base over acid, [A⁻] / [HA].

## Check questions
1. What two things make an acidic buffer?
   Answer: A weak acid and its salt (its conjugate base).
2. A buffer has [A⁻] ten times [HA]. How does pH compare with pKa?
   Answer: pH = pKa + 1, because log 10 = 1.
3. Why does adding a little acid barely change the pH?
   Answer: A⁻ mops up the extra H⁺ and turns into HA.

## Flashcards
Q: What does a buffer do?
A: It keeps pH nearly steady when small amounts of acid or base are added.
Q: What is the pH when [HA] = [A⁻]?
A: pH equals pKa.
Q: What is pKa?
A: Minus the log of Ka.
Q: Which part of a buffer removes added acid?
A: The conjugate base, A⁻.
Q: Which part removes added alkali?
A: The weak acid, HA.

## Root check
- ph-scale: What is the pH of a neutral solution at 25 °C? Answer: 7.
- equilibrium-constant: Write Kc for N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃. Answer: Kc = [NH₃]² / ([N₂][H₂]³).
