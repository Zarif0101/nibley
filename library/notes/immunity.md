# Immunity

## Simple idea
Germs such as bacteria and viruses are attacked by your white blood cells. Each germ has marker molecules called antigens. White blood cells make antibodies that lock onto one antigen, like a key in a lock, and mark the germ for destruction. After you recover, memory cells stay. A vaccine trains them without making you ill.

## Key formulas
- Antigen + matching antibody → germ is marked and destroyed
- Vaccine → memory cells made → fast response if the real germ arrives
- Each antibody fits only one antigen

## Picture it
Draw `bacterium` and write "antigen" on its `bacterium.cell-wall`. Draw `antibody` beside it, with `antibody.binding-site` touching the bacterium, labelled "fits the antigen".

## Common mistakes
- Thinking antibiotics work on viruses: antibiotics only kill bacteria.
- Thinking a vaccine gives you the disease: it uses a harmless piece or weakened germ.
- Thinking one antibody fits every germ: each fits one specific antigen.

## Check questions
1. What do antibodies attach to?
   Answer: Antigens on a germ.
2. Why is a second infection usually milder?
   Answer: Memory cells make antibodies quickly.
3. Why do vaccines protect without causing illness?
   Answer: They show the immune system the antigen safely, so it learns to respond.

## Flashcards
Q: What is an antigen?
A: A marker molecule on a germ that triggers immunity.
Q: What is an antibody?
A: A protein that locks onto a specific antigen.
Q: Which cells make antibodies?
A: White blood cells called B lymphocytes.
Q: What is a vaccine?
A: A harmless form of a germ that trains immunity.
Q: What do phagocytes do?
A: They engulf and digest germs.
