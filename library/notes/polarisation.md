# Polarisation

## Simple idea
Shake a rope through a fence with vertical slats: up-down shakes pass, side-to-side shakes are blocked. A polariser does this to light. It lets through vibrations in one direction only. Polarising sunglasses cut glare this way. Only transverse waves can be polarised.

## Key formulas
- I = I₀·cos²θ. Malus's law. I: intensity after the filter, I₀: intensity before (watts per m², W/m²), θ: angle between the light's direction of vibration and the filter's (degrees)
- Unpolarised light through one polariser: I = I₀ / 2

## Picture it
Draw `polariser`. Label `polariser.wave-in` "vibrates every way". Label `polariser.slits` "only one direction passes". Label `polariser.wave-out` "one direction only".

## Common mistakes
- Thinking sound can be polarised: sound is longitudinal, so it cannot.
- Using cosθ without squaring: intensity uses cos²θ.
- Expecting zero light from one filter: unpolarised light loses only half.

## Check questions
1. Unpolarised light of intensity 80 W/m² passes one polariser. What intensity comes out?
   Answer: 40 W/m².
2. Polarised light of 80 W/m² hits a filter at 60° to its direction. What comes out?
   Answer: 20 W/m², because cos²60° = 0.25.
3. Why does polarisation show that light is a transverse wave?
   Answer: Only waves that vibrate sideways to their travel can be filtered to one direction.

## Flashcards
Q: What is polarised light?
A: Light whose waves vibrate in only one direction.
Q: Which waves can be polarised?
A: Transverse waves only.
Q: Name one use of polarisation.
A: Polarising sunglasses, or LCD screens.
Q: What happens when two filters are crossed at 90°?
A: No light gets through.
Q: What is Malus's law?
A: I = I₀·cos²θ.
