# Amines and amino acids

## Simple idea
Ammonia is a base because the nitrogen has a spare pair of electrons that grabs H⁺. Swap a hydrogen for a carbon chain and you get an amine, which does the same. An amino acid has an amine end and an acid end on one molecule, so it can act as both. Proteins are chains of them.

## Key formulas
- R–NH₂ + H⁺ → R–NH₃⁺. Amine takes H⁺, so it is a base
- Amino acid: H₂N–CHR–COOH. R: side chain (for glycine, R = H)
- Zwitterion: ⁺H₃N–CHR–COO⁻ (both charges, overall neutral)
- Two amino acids join: –COOH + H₂N– → –CO–NH– + H₂O

## Picture it
Draw `carbon-chain`. Label `carbon-chain.group` "–NH₂ (base end)" and `carbon-chain.chain-end` "–COOH (acid end)".
Circle the –CO–NH– link between two chains and label it "peptide bond".

## Common mistakes
- Calling amines acids: the N lone pair takes H⁺, so they are bases.
- Forgetting water leaves when a peptide bond forms.
- Thinking an amino acid has only one charge form: it changes with pH.

## Check questions
1. Which group makes a molecule an amine?
   Answer: –NH₂ (a nitrogen with a lone pair).
2. How many peptide bonds are in a chain of three amino acids?
   Answer: Two.
3. Why can an amino acid react with both acids and bases?
   Answer: The –NH₂ end takes H⁺ and the –COOH end gives H⁺.

## Flashcards
Q: What is the name of the bond linking amino acids?
A: The peptide bond, –CO–NH–.
Q: What is a zwitterion?
A: An ion with both a positive and a negative charge, overall neutral.
Q: What is glycine's side chain R?
A: Just a hydrogen atom.
Q: What does the nitrogen lone pair do in an amine?
A: It accepts H⁺.
Q: What small molecule is lost when two amino acids join?
A: Water.
