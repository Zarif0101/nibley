# Benzene and aromatic compounds

## Simple idea
Benzene is a flat ring of six carbons. Instead of three fixed double bonds, six electrons float around the whole ring like a loop of cars on a ring road. That makes the ring very stable. So benzene prefers to swap a hydrogen for another group and keep its ring, rather than adding things across it.

## Key formulas
- C₆H₆. Benzene: six carbons, six hydrogens
- C₆H₆ + HNO₃ → C₆H₅NO₂ + H₂O. Nitration: warm conc. HNO₃ with conc. H₂SO₄ catalyst
- C₆H₆ + Br₂ → C₆H₅Br + HBr. Needs a halogen carrier catalyst such as FeBr₃

## Common mistakes
- Drawing alternating single and double bonds as real: all six bonds are the same length.
- Expecting bromine water to go colourless with benzene: it does not, because the ring resists addition.
- Writing addition products: benzene reacts by substitution.

## Check questions
1. How many carbon atoms and electrons in the delocalised ring?
   Answer: Six carbons and six delocalised electrons.
2. What forms when benzene reacts with a nitrating mixture?
   Answer: Nitrobenzene (and water).
3. Why does benzene prefer substitution to addition?
   Answer: Substitution keeps the stable delocalised ring; addition would break it.

## Flashcards
Q: What is the molecular formula of benzene?
A: C₆H₆.
Q: What does delocalised mean here?
A: Electrons are shared around the whole ring, not fixed in one bond.
Q: What happens to bromine water when shaken with benzene?
A: Nothing: it stays orange.
Q: What is the shape of the benzene molecule?
A: Flat (planar) hexagon.
Q: What type of reaction is typical of benzene?
A: Substitution.

## Root check
- hydrocarbons: Does bromine water turn colourless with an alkene? Answer: Yes.
- covalent-bonding: How many electrons are shared in a double bond? Answer: Four.
