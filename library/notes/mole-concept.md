# The mole

## Simple idea
Atoms are far too small to count one by one. So chemists count in big bundles, like a dozen eggs. One mole is 6.02 × 10²³ particles. The useful trick is that a mole of any substance weighs its formula mass in grams, so you can count particles by weighing.

## Key formulas
- n = m / M. n: amount (mol), m: mass (g), M: molar mass (g/mol)
- N = n × Nₐ. N: number of particles, Nₐ: Avogadro constant, 6.02 × 10²³ /mol
- M(H₂O) = 2 × 1 + 16 = 18 g/mol
- M(CO₂) = 12 + 2 × 16 = 44 g/mol

## Common mistakes
- Mixing up mass and moles: divide the mass by M to get moles.
- Using 16 for O₂: it has 2 atoms, so M = 32 g/mol.
- Dropping the units: M is in g/mol, so a mass in kg must become g first.

## Check questions
1. How many moles are in 36 g of water (M = 18 g/mol)?
   Answer: 2 mol.
2. What is the mass of 0.5 mol of CO₂ (M = 44 g/mol)?
   Answer: 22 g.
3. Why do we use the mole instead of counting atoms?
   Answer: Atoms are too tiny to count, but moles link easy-to-measure mass to particle number.

## Flashcards
Q: How many particles are in one mole?
A: 6.02 × 10²³.
Q: What is the symbol of the Avogadro constant?
A: Nₐ, which is 6.02 × 10²³ /mol.
Q: What is the formula for moles from mass?
A: n = m / M.
Q: What are the units of molar mass?
A: Grams per mole, g/mol.
Q: What is the molar mass of carbon dioxide, CO₂?
A: 44 g/mol.
