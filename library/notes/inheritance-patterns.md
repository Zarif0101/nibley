# Codominance and sex linkage

## Simple idea
Sometimes both alleles show at once. In codominance, a red and a white allele can give a red-and-white flower. Other genes sit on the X chromosome. Males (XY) have only one X, so one recessive allele on it will show. Females (XX) need two.

## Key formulas
- Codominance: allele A + allele B → both show (blood group AB)
- Female = XX, male = XY
- X-linked cross: carrier mother (X^H X^h) × normal father (X^H Y) → half the sons affected (1 in 4 of all children)
- Each child has a 1 in 2 chance of being male

## Picture it
Draw `chromosome`. Label `chromosome.centromere` "X chromosome" and `chromosome.arm-top-left` "gene with allele". Then draw `punnett-square` with "X^H" and "X^h" along `punnett-square.top-1` and `punnett-square.top-2`, and "X^H" and "Y" along `punnett-square.side-1` and `punnett-square.side-2`.

## Common mistakes
- Treating codominance as blending: both alleles show fully, side by side.
- Thinking colour blindness is equal in both sexes: males show it more often, as they have one X.
- Thinking a father passes an X-linked allele to his sons: sons get his Y.

## Check questions
1. Which sex chromosomes does a male have?
   Answer: X and Y.
2. Blood group: one A allele and one B allele. What group?
   Answer: AB, because both show (codominance).
3. Why are X-linked conditions more common in males?
   Answer: Males have one X, so one recessive allele is enough to show.

## Flashcards
Q: What is codominance?
A: Both alleles show fully in the heterozygote.
Q: What are the female sex chromosomes?
A: XX.
Q: What is a carrier?
A: Someone with one recessive allele who does not show the condition.
Q: Name one X-linked condition.
A: Red-green colour blindness.
Q: From whom does a son get his X chromosome?
A: His mother.
