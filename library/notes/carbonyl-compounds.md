# Aldehydes and ketones

## Simple idea
Both have a carbon double-bonded to oxygen, C=O. In an aldehyde that C=O sits at the end of the chain, so the carbon still has a hydrogen on it. In a ketone it sits in the middle. That spare hydrogen is why aldehydes are easily oxidised and ketones are not.

## Key formulas
- Aldehyde: R–CHO. Ketone: R–CO–R. R: a carbon chain
- Aldehyde + oxidiser → carboxylic acid. Ketone: no reaction
- Aldehyde + NaBH₄ → primary alcohol. Ketone + NaBH₄ → secondary alcohol
- Tollens' reagent: aldehyde gives a silver mirror

## Picture it
Draw `carbon-chain`. Label `carbon-chain.chain-end` "aldehyde: C=O at the end".
On a second `carbon-chain`, label `carbon-chain.carbon-2` "ketone: C=O in the middle".

## Common mistakes
- Mixing up the two by name: ethanal is an aldehyde, propanone is a ketone.
- Thinking ketones oxidise like aldehydes: they give no change with Tollens' or dichromate.
- Reducing to the wrong alcohol: aldehydes give primary, ketones give secondary.

## Check questions
1. Which has C=O at the end of the chain, an aldehyde or a ketone?
   Answer: An aldehyde.
2. What do you see when an aldehyde is warmed with Tollens' reagent?
   Answer: A silver mirror forms.
3. Why does a ketone resist oxidation?
   Answer: Its C=O carbon has no hydrogen on it, so it cannot be oxidised easily.

## Flashcards
Q: What group do aldehydes and ketones share?
A: The carbonyl group, C=O.
Q: Name the simplest ketone.
A: Propanone.
Q: What does oxidising an aldehyde make?
A: A carboxylic acid.
Q: What colour change does dichromate show with an aldehyde?
A: Orange to green.
Q: What does NaBH₄ do to a ketone?
A: Reduces it to a secondary alcohol.

## Root check
- alcohols: What forms when a primary alcohol is gently oxidised? Answer: An aldehyde.
