# Activation energy and catalysts

## Simple idea
To push a boulder into the next valley, you first push it up a hill. That first push is the activation energy: the minimum energy particles need to react. A catalyst is like a tunnel through the hill. It makes the push smaller without changing where you end up.

## Key formulas
- Eₐ: activation energy (kJ/mol), the minimum energy needed to react
- ΔH = energy of products − energy of reactants. ΔH: enthalpy change (kJ/mol)
- A catalyst lowers Eₐ but ΔH stays the same

## Picture it
Draw `energy-profile`. Arrow from `energy-profile.reactants` up to `energy-profile.peak`, labelled "activation energy". Arrow up to `energy-profile.catalyst-peak`, labelled "lower with catalyst". Label `energy-profile.delta-h` "ΔH, unchanged".

## Common mistakes
- Saying a catalyst changes ΔH: it only lowers the hill, not the start or end.
- Saying a catalyst is used up: it comes out unchanged.
- Mixing up Eₐ and ΔH: Eₐ is up to the hill top, ΔH is start to end.

## Check questions
1. What does a catalyst do to the activation energy?
   Answer: It lowers it.
2. Does a catalyst change ΔH?
   Answer: No, ΔH stays the same.
3. Why does a catalyst speed up a reaction?
   Answer: More particles have enough energy to react, so more collisions succeed.

## Flashcards
Q: What is activation energy?
A: The minimum energy particles need for a collision to react.
Q: What is a catalyst?
A: A substance that speeds up a reaction and is not used up.
Q: How does a catalyst work?
A: It gives a different path with lower activation energy.
Q: Does a catalyst change the products made?
A: No, it only changes how fast they form.
Q: Which direction does a hump go on an exothermic profile?
A: Up from reactants, then down to lower products.

## Root check
- reaction-rates: Why does a hotter reaction go faster? Answer: Particles collide more often and with more energy.
- enthalpy-changes: Is a reaction with ΔH = −50 kJ/mol exothermic or endothermic? Answer: Exothermic, it gives out heat.
