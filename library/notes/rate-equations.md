# Rate equations and order

## Simple idea
A rate equation tells you how much each ingredient controls the speed. If doubling one ingredient doubles the speed, it is first order. If it quadruples the speed, it is second order. If nothing changes, it is zero order.

## Key formulas
- rate = k[A]ᵐ[B]ⁿ. rate: mol/dm³/s; k: rate constant; [A], [B]: concentrations (mol/dm³); m, n: orders
- Overall order = m + n
- Units of k: s⁻¹ for first order overall; dm³/(mol·s) for second order overall

## Picture it
Draw `graph-line` with "rate" up the side and "[A]" along the bottom. Label `graph-line.line-end` "first order: rate rises steadily".

## Common mistakes
- Reading orders from the balanced equation: orders come from experiments only.
- Thinking a bigger order means a bigger k: k is a separate constant.
- Forgetting k changes with temperature: it rises when it gets hotter.

## Check questions
1. Rate = k[A]. [A] is doubled. What happens to the rate?
   Answer: It doubles.
2. Rate = k[A]², k = 0.5 dm³/(mol·s), [A] = 0.2 mol/dm³. What is the rate?
   Answer: 0.5 × 0.04 = 0.02 mol/dm³/s.
3. Why can zero order in B mean B does not change the rate?
   Answer: [B]⁰ = 1, so B drops out of the equation.

## Flashcards
Q: What is the general rate equation for A and B?
A: rate = k[A]ᵐ[B]ⁿ.
Q: What does second order in A mean?
A: Doubling [A] makes the rate four times bigger.
Q: How do you find the order?
A: From experiments, not from the balanced equation.
Q: What does k stand for?
A: The rate constant.
Q: What is the overall order?
A: The sum of all the orders.
