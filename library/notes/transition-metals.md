# Transition metals and complex ions

## Simple idea
Iron, copper and chromium sit in the middle block of the periodic table. Their ions make bright colours, like blue copper sulfate. They can lose different numbers of electrons, so one metal gives several ions. They also hold molecules around themselves like a crowd, forming complex ions.

## Key formulas
- [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺. Complex ion: Cu²⁺ centre with 6 water ligands (each donates a lone pair)
- Charge on complex = metal charge + sum of ligand charges
- Cu²⁺(aq) + excess NH₃ → [Cu(NH₃)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺ (deep blue)

## Common mistakes
- Counting every d-block element: scandium and zinc do not count, as their ions have an empty or full d subshell.
- Forgetting ligands count in the charge: water is neutral, but Cl⁻ is 1−.
- Thinking colour comes from the metal alone: ligands change the colour too.

## Check questions
1. Name two properties of transition metals.
   Answer: Coloured ions and more than one oxidation state (also good catalysts).
2. What is the oxidation state of iron in [Fe(H₂O)₆]³⁺?
   Answer: +3, because water is neutral.
3. Why is a ligand able to bond to the metal ion?
   Answer: It has a lone pair of electrons to share with the metal.

## Flashcards
Q: What is a ligand?
A: A molecule or ion that donates a lone pair to a metal ion.
Q: What colour is Cu²⁺ in water?
A: Blue.
Q: Name a transition metal used as a catalyst.
A: Iron in the Haber process.
Q: What is the coordination number of [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺?
A: 6.
Q: Which block of the periodic table are transition metals in?
A: The d block.

## Root check
- electron-configuration: How many electrons can a d subshell hold? Answer: 10.
- periodic-table-trends: Across a period, does atomic radius grow or shrink? Answer: It shrinks.
- redox: Is a species that gains electrons oxidised or reduced? Answer: Reduced.
