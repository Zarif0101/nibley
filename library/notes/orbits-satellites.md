# Gravitational fields and orbits

## Simple idea
A satellite is always falling toward the Earth, but it also moves sideways so fast that the ground curves away beneath it. So it keeps falling around the planet and never lands. Gravity is the force that bends its path into a circle.

## Key formulas
- g = G·M / r². g: field strength (N/kg), G: 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg², M: planet's mass (kg), r: distance from the centre (m)
- v = √(G·M / r). v: orbital speed (m/s)
- v = 2π·r / T. T: time for one orbit (s)

## Common mistakes
- Thinking astronauts float because there is no gravity: they are in free fall.
- Thinking a heavier satellite needs a higher speed: mass cancels out.
- Using height above the surface as r: use the distance from the planet's centre.

## Check questions
1. What force keeps a satellite in orbit?
   Answer: Gravity, acting as the centripetal force.
2. A satellite travels a circle of length 4.0 × 10⁷ m at 8000 m/s. What is its period?
   Answer: 4.0 × 10⁷ ÷ 8000 = 5000 s.
3. Why does a satellite in a higher orbit move more slowly?
   Answer: Gravity is weaker farther away, and v = √(GM / r) falls as r grows.

## Flashcards
Q: What is a gravitational field?
A: A region where a mass feels a gravity force.
Q: What is the unit of field strength?
A: N/kg, the same as m/s².
Q: What gives a satellite its centripetal force?
A: Gravity from the planet.
Q: Distance from the centre doubles. What happens to g?
A: It becomes one quarter.
Q: Does orbital speed depend on the satellite's mass?
A: No. It depends on the planet's mass and the radius.

## Root check
- gravitation: Distance between two masses triples. What happens to the force? Answer: It becomes one ninth.
- circular-motion: Which way does the net force point on an object moving in a circle? Answer: Toward the centre.
