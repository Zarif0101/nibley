# Hess's law

## Simple idea
Climbing to a hilltop by the steep path or the long winding path, you gain the same height. Reactions work like that. The total energy change from start to finish is the same whichever route you take, so you can add up smaller steps.

## Key formulas
- ΔH(route 1) = ΔH(route 2). ΔH: enthalpy change (kJ/mol)
- ΔH(total) = ΔH₁ + ΔH₂ + … for steps in a chain. Each ΔH in kJ/mol
- Flip a reaction around: its ΔH changes sign. Double a reaction: double its ΔH

## Picture it
Draw `energy-profile`. Mark `energy-profile.reactants` "start" and `energy-profile.products` "end". Put an arrow along `energy-profile.delta-h` labelled "total ΔH, any route".

## Common mistakes
- Forgetting to flip the sign when a reaction is reversed: reverse it, then change + to − (or − to +).
- Not scaling ΔH when you double the equation: multiply ΔH by the same number.
- Adding up steps that do not cancel to the target: check that extra substances cancel out.

## Check questions
1. A → B has ΔH = −100 kJ/mol and B → C has ΔH = −50 kJ/mol. What is ΔH for A → C?
   Answer: −150 kJ/mol.
2. X → Y has ΔH = +40 kJ/mol. What is ΔH for Y → X?
   Answer: −40 kJ/mol.
3. Why can we find ΔH for a reaction we cannot run in the lab?
   Answer: Energy change depends only on start and end, so we add up steps we do know.

## Flashcards
Q: State Hess's law in words.
A: Total enthalpy change is the same whatever route the reaction takes.
Q: What happens to ΔH when you reverse a reaction?
A: It keeps its size but changes sign.
Q: A reaction is doubled. What happens to ΔH?
A: It doubles.
Q: What does a negative ΔH tell you?
A: The reaction is exothermic and gives out heat.
Q: What is the unit of ΔH?
A: Kilojoules per mole, kJ/mol.

## Root check
- enthalpy-changes: Is a reaction with ΔH = −50 kJ/mol exothermic or endothermic? Answer: Exothermic, it gives out heat.
