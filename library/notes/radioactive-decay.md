# Radioactive decay and half-life

## Simple idea
Some nuclei are unstable and, at a random moment, break up and give off radiation. You cannot tell when one nucleus will go, but a big group behaves predictably. Half-life is the time for half of them to decay, like a pile of sweets halving each round.

## Key formulas
- N = N₀·(½)^(t / T). N: nuclei left (count), N₀: starting nuclei, t: time passed, T: half-life (same time unit as t)
- Alpha: nucleus loses 2 protons and 2 neutrons. Beta-minus: a neutron turns into a proton. Gamma: energy only, protons and neutrons stay the same

## Common mistakes
- Thinking two half-lives remove everything: each half-life halves what is left, so a quarter remains.
- Thinking you can predict one nucleus: only the group's behaviour is predictable.
- Mixing up the types: alpha is heavy and stopped by paper, gamma goes through a lot.

## Check questions
1. A sample of 80 g has a half-life of 3 days. How much is left after 6 days?
   Answer: 20 g.
2. After 3 half-lives, what fraction is left?
   Answer: One eighth.
3. Why can we not say when one particular nucleus will decay?
   Answer: Decay is random; only the chance per second is fixed.

## Flashcards
Q: What is half-life?
A: The time for half the unstable nuclei to decay.
Q: What is an alpha particle?
A: Two protons and two neutrons, a helium nucleus.
Q: Which radiation is stopped by paper?
A: Alpha.
Q: Which radiation has no mass and no charge?
A: Gamma.
Q: Does temperature change a nucleus's half-life?
A: No, it stays the same.

## Root check
- nuclear-structure: Carbon-12 has 6 protons. How many neutrons? Answer: 6.
- exponential-functions: A quantity of 80 halves every hour. What is left after 3 hours? Answer: 10.
