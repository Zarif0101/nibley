# Expanding and factorising

## Simple idea
Expanding multiplies out brackets: 3(x + 2) means 3 lots of x and 3 lots of 2, so 3x + 6. Factorising goes backwards, like turning 12 back into 3 × 4. Spot what both parts share, here 3, and write it outside the brackets: 3(x + 2).

## Key formulas
- a(b + c) = ab + ac. a, b, c: any numbers
- (x + a)(x + b) = x² + (a + b)x + ab. a, b: numbers
- x² − a² = (x − a)(x + a). a: a number

## Picture it
On note paper write 3(x + 2) on the left, an arrow to 3x + 6 on the right labelled "expand", and the arrow back labelled "factorise".
Draw a rectangle 3 tall, its width split into x and 2, with areas "3x" and "6".

## Common mistakes
- Multiplying only the first term: 3(x + 2) is 3x + 6, not 3x + 2.
- Squaring wrongly: (x + 3)² is x² + 6x + 9, not x² + 9.
- Not taking out the biggest common number: 6x + 9 is 3(2x + 3).

## Check questions
1. Is factorising putting brackets in or taking them away?
   Answer: Putting brackets back in.
2. Expand 2(x + 5).
   Answer: 2x + 10.
3. Why is it smart to check by expanding?
   Answer: Expanding should bring you back to where you started.

## Flashcards
Q: Expand 3(x − 1).
A: 3x − 3.
Q: Factorise 5x + 10.
A: 5(x + 2).
Q: Expand (x + 1)(x + 2).
A: x² + 3x + 2.
Q: Factorise x² + 5x + 6.
A: (x + 2)(x + 3).
Q: Factorise x² − 9.
A: (x − 3)(x + 3).
