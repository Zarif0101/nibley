# Resistance and Ohm's law

## Simple idea
Think of water in a pipe. A narrow, long pipe lets less water through for the same push. A wire does the same to current. That holding back is resistance. More voltage gives more current, and more resistance gives less current. For many wires and resistors, the voltage divided by the current stays the same.

## Key formulas
- V = I·R. V: voltage (volts, V), I: current (amperes, A), R: resistance (ohms, Ω)
- I = V / R. The same law, solved for current
- R = ρ·L / A. ρ: resistivity of the material (Ω·m), L: length (m), A: cross-section area (m²)

## Common mistakes
- Thinking resistance depends on the voltage you apply: for a normal resistor, R stays fixed.
- Mixing up which quantity to find: write V = I·R first, then rearrange.
- Thinking a thicker wire has more resistance: thicker means less.

## Check questions
1. 12 V is across a 4 Ω resistor. What is the current?
   Answer: I = 12 / 4 = 3 A.
2. A 2 A current flows through 5 Ω. What is the voltage?
   Answer: V = 2 × 5 = 10 V.
3. Why does a longer wire have more resistance?
   Answer: R = ρL/A, so more length means more R; charge has more to push through.

## Flashcards
Q: State Ohm's law.
A: V = I·R.
Q: What is the unit of resistance?
A: The ohm (Ω), equal to one volt per ampere.
Q: Voltage doubles across a fixed resistor. What happens to current?
A: It doubles.
Q: What happens to R if wire length doubles?
A: It doubles.
Q: What happens to R if wire area doubles?
A: It halves.

## Root check
- electric-current: 10 C passes a point in 5 s. What is the current? Answer: 2 A.
- electric-potential: A battery gives each coulomb 9 J of energy. What is its voltage? Answer: 9 V.
