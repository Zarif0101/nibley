# Electric field

## Simple idea
Think of wind: you cannot see it, but it pushes anything you put in it. An electric field is like that around a charge. Put a small test charge anywhere and the field tells you how hard it gets pushed, per coulomb. Field lines point the way a positive charge would be pushed.

## Key formulas
- E = F / q. E: electric field (newtons per coulomb, N/C), F: force on the test charge (N), q: test charge (C)
- E = k·Q / r². Field of a single point charge. Q: source charge (C), r: distance (m), k ≈ 9.0 × 10⁹ N·m²/C²
- E = V / d. Field between two plates. V: voltage between them (volts, V), d: gap (m)

## Common mistakes
- Pointing field lines into a positive charge: they leave positive and enter negative.
- Mixing up E and F: E is force per coulomb, so F = q·E.
- Using r instead of r² in E = kQ/r²: square the distance.

## Check questions
1. A 4 × 10⁻⁶ C charge feels a force of 0.02 N. What is the field there?
   Answer: E = 0.02 / (4 × 10⁻⁶) = 5000 N/C.
2. A 2 × 10⁻⁹ C charge, 0.3 m away. What is its field there?
   Answer: E = 9.0 × 10⁹ × 2 × 10⁻⁹ / 0.09 = 200 N/C.
3. Why are the lines between two parallel plates evenly spaced?
   Answer: The field has the same strength and direction everywhere in the gap.

## Flashcards
Q: What is an electric field?
A: The force per coulomb a charge would feel at a point.
Q: Which way do field lines point?
A: The way a positive charge would be pushed.
Q: What is the unit of electric field?
A: Newtons per coulomb (N/C), the same as volts per metre.
Q: What does closely packed field lines mean?
A: A stronger field.
Q: How do you find the force on a charge q in field E?
A: F = q·E.

## Root check
